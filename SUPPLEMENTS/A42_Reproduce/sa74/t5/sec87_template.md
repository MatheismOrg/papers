### 8.7 The known laws on a supplied lattice

Section 7 points to a candidate from the top: supply a four-dimensional lattice as data, run the known laws on it, and let general relativity bend it. This section writes the candidate out, completes it, tests the lattice forms it needs and counts its bits with the compressor of Section 7 (Table 12; supplements A42_SA74_Supplied_Lattice.py, A42_SA74_Forms.py and A42_SA74_Regge.c).

**Lattice form.** The lattice is hypercubic with the Planck length as its spacing, and it is physical, with no continuum limit to reach. Gauge fields become phases on its links (Wilson, 1974), and the metric becomes the lengths of the edges of a triangulation, so general relativity acts through the Regge action (Regge, 1961). Naive lattice fermions come with doublers (Nielsen and Ninomiya, 1981); the overlap operator (Neuberger, 1998) removes them and keeps an exact lattice chiral symmetry, the Ginsparg-Wilson relation (Ginsparg and Wilson, 1982; Lüscher, 1998). On a two-dimensional U(1) lattice the naive operator has {naive} zero modes in the free field where the continuum has 2, and in {nrows} backgrounds of topological charge -2 to 3, smooth and rough (plaquette angles up to {pmax} radians), the overlap operator's index equals the charge every time while the relation holds to {gw}. That settles the strong force. The weak force acts on left-handed fields only. Its lattice form exists for U(1) (Lüscher, 1999) and is proposed for the full Standard Model (Wang and Wen, 2020), against objections (Golterman and Shamir, 2023, 2026). In 1+1 dimensions the route has worked numerically: in the ungauged 3-4-5-0 model, whose chirality lies in its charge assignments, the mirror sector gaps without breaking the symmetry while the light sector stays gapless (Zeng, Zhu, Wang and You, 2022). Gauged versions have been proposed (Wang, 2022), but a numerical test with a fluctuating gauge field, and a selective mirror gap in 3+1 dimensions, are still open. Simplicity favors that route: a local lattice rule gives both handednesses in equal numbers, so a short rule keeps both and makes the mirror hand heavy at the lattice scale without breaking the symmetries, an echo of the mirror particles that Lee and Yang (1956) proposed. The anomalies must cancel, including the mod-16 anomaly of 5(B - L) - 4Y: the statement's 16 Weyl fermions per family cancel them all, where 15 would leave {m15} (García-Etxebarria and Montero, 2019). The weak force is weak, so a program can compute its effects in perturbation theory to any measured precision, and the cap below holds however this ends.

**Lorentz symmetry.** A lattice that treats its four directions alike forbids by hypercubic symmetry the terms that would give particles different top speeds, and its time step is the transfer matrix along one direction; a step that treats time apart, like that of Section 8.1, would need one tuned speed per particle type (Collins et al., 2004). The photon then has $E = p - a^2p^3/12$ along an axis and $E = p - a^2p^3/18$ along a body diagonal, so LHAASO's limit on quadratic dispersion, $E_{{QG,2}} > 6.9 \times 10^{{11}}$ GeV (Cao et al., 2024), allows spacings up to {amax_m} m, about {amax_lp} million Planck lengths.

**Completion.** The Standard Model with general relativity lacks dark matter and an initial state. The shortest completion declares them: a free scalar with a declared mass, and five numbers for the initial state. SMASH (Ballesteros, Redondo, Ringwald and Tamarit, 2017) derives them instead, from an axion, a heavy quark and Majorana neutrino masses, and predicts a tensor-to-scalar ratio of at least 0.004 and an axion of 50 to 200 μeV; it costs {dsmash} more bits and would earn them only if those predictions are measured. Dark energy may need no entry: in the timescape model clocks run faster in emptier regions, so the averaged expansion only appears to accelerate (Wiltshire, 2007), which a recent supernova analysis mildly favors (Seifert, Lane, Galoppo, Ridden-Harper and Wiltshire, 2025) against arguments that such effects are too small (Green and Wald, 2014); then Λ is zero. Inputs may also become rules at the lattice scale: assuming asymptotically safe gravity drives the Higgs self-coupling close to zero there, Shaposhnikov and Wetterich (2010) predicted a Higgs mass near 126 GeV before its discovery, against 125.2 measured.

**What the lattice leaves open.** A physical lattice makes the path integral finite at the price of fine-tuning: the bare cosmological constant must cancel the vacuum energy of the lattice's fields to about {dlam} decimal places, and the bare Higgs mass parameter its shift to about {dhig}. As numbers those digits would cost {blam} and {bhig} bits; as the condition that the measured values result, as the statement writes them, they cost only that condition, so fine-tuning, like the strong CP problem's θ = 0, costs no bits. Every measurement lies far below the lattice scale, where general relativity as an effective quantum theory gives finite predictions (Donoghue, 1994), so the statement covers present data. Joining general relativity with quantum theory at black-hole centers and the first instants of expansion turns on gravity at the lattice's own scale, where the Euclidean action is unbounded below (Gibbons, Hawking and Perry, 1978). That test has begun. On a fixed 16⁴ Regge lattice Hamber and Williams (1995) found an attractive potential near a critical point, consistent with Newton's law within large errors; dynamical triangulations recover Newtonian binding within 20 to 30 percent, without matter acting back (Dai, Laiho, Schiffer and Unmuth-Yockey, 2021); and causal triangulations, which glue four-dimensional blocks causally, find a four-dimensional de Sitter universe (Ambjørn, Jurkiewicz and Loll, 2004; Ambjørn, Görlich, Jurkiewicz and Loll, 2008). On the supplied lattice itself, 4⁴ sites with λ = 1 and a uniform measure, the supplement finds a smooth phase after a flat start whose deficit angles are below $2 \times 10^{{-15}}$: the average curvature is negative and shrinks from -1.84 at k = 0 to -0.77 at k = 0.065, and above k ≈ 0.07 the lattice degenerates, near the critical value $k_c = 0.0639$ that Hamber (2015) finds on lattices up to 32⁴ with exponent $\nu = 0.334(4)$. On lattices this small the curvature does not reach zero first, so the run locates the edge of the smooth phase, not the critical point. Positive curvature-squared terms extend the smooth phase (Hamber, 1992), and Hamber (2019) proposes that the observed cosmological constant is set by the correlation length near the critical point, $\Lambda \approx 3/\xi^2$, which leaves that length to be fixed.

**Matter on causal triangulations.** Fermions there need frames on a changing geometry, and each change alters their determinant. Written as differential forms, Kähler-Dirac fields, they need no frames (Kähler, 1962; Becher and Joos, 1982; Catterall, Laiho and Unmuth-Yockey, 2018). On six sampled two-dimensional causal triangulations of a torus (Ambjørn and Loll, 1998) such a field has exactly 4 zero modes, the torus's Betti numbers, and low modes 0.92 to 1.10 times Weyl's law, so no doubled modes; on the supplied four-dimensional lattice, bent by random weights, its zero modes number 16. Each such field carries four copies of a Dirac fermion. Catterall (2021) proposed interactions that would leave 16 Weyl fermions if they gap one half and the continuum limit holds, tested in two dimensions, and four-dimensional gauge theories show symmetric gapped phases (Hasenfratz, 2022; Butt, Catterall and Hasenfratz, 2025), though none yet leaves the Standard Model's chiral spectrum. Since the Standard Model's masses lie far below the lattice scale, its fields act back on the geometry mainly through their numbers of each spin, so a first test, a proxy for the full matter sector, needs only free fields in those numbers.

**The cap.** Written out in full (the statement, its lattice form and the declared completion), the candidate compresses to {cap} bits, an upper bound in this encoding on a complete statement of the known laws with dark matter and an initial state. An upper bound is what this route can prove. The search from the bottom proves lower bounds only within the classes it runs (Sections 4 to 6), and no program can be proved the shortest for its output once its length exceeds a constant set by the axioms used (Chaitin, 1974). A theory of everything can therefore be capped from the top, bounded from below within classes and tested against data; it cannot be proved minimal.
