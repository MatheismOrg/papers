## Research Note: Do Points Exist? The Geometry of Structure-Without-Stuff

**Observation chain:**
1. Circles can't be instantiated — zero-width boundary can't interact with anything
2. But neither can cubes (zero-width edges, zero-dimensional vertices)
3. Any geometric shape has this property — exact mathematical definition involves zero-dimensional elements
4. Discrete graphs seem to escape, but nodes are... points. Same problem.
5. Numbers are positions, but positions in what? If in a continuum (number line), back to zero-width. If discrete, the discrete elements are... points again.

**The apparent trap:** every mathematical object reduces to something with the "can't fully instantiate" property. Points, lines, surfaces — all have zero-measure boundaries at some level.

**Existing math theory:**
- **Category theory:** Objects defined entirely by morphisms (relationships). Points are where arrows meet. Never asks "what is a point made of." Relations are primitive.
- **Structuralism (Shapiro, Resnik):** Objects ARE positions in structures. Nothing underneath.
- **Homotopy Type Theory:** Paths more fundamental than points. Points are where paths meet.
- **Intuitionism (Brouwer):** Only constructive existence. Avoids continuum problems but loses much math.

**Possible matheist resolution:** "What are points made of" is a stuff-question applied to a structure-world. Nothing is made of anything. Relations are primitive. The "point" is a name for where relationships converge. This IS the structural realism claim, but arrived at through geometry rather than philosophy.

**Spectrum of "existence":**
- Fully consistent math (2+2=4): generates unlimited further structure
- Consistent as constraint (circle, cube): constrains relationships (inside/outside) but can't be "built"
- Consistent as description only (2+2=5): exists as pattern, can't enter productive relationships under own definitions

**Key insight:** even fully consistent math exists as relationships, not as things. The question "what are the nodes" dissolves the same way "what is the electron made of" dissolves in QFT — the field IS the thing, the particle is an excitation pattern, not a ball of stuff.

**Connection to existing portfolio:** strengthens the structural realism passages in Nous, FoP, P7 (constructor theory), and Part 1. Currently argues "no stuff, only structure" abstractly. This geometric route makes it concrete: even a circle can't be a thing. Nothing can. Everything exists as relationships. The question is how many productive relationships each can enter.

**Status:** 80% formed. Needs more development before adding to submission files. One sentence in Part 1 near structural realism may be appropriate. Full treatment could be a new paper or extension of P7.

**Open question:** does this mean discrete and continuous are both "the same kind of non-instantiable"? If so, the discrete vs continuous debate dissolves into "which relational structure has shorter specification" — which is already the SDP argument. The geometric route arrives at the same conclusion by a different path.

## Research Note: The Missing Level — Fundamental Randomness and K

**Core observation (from session):**
With discrete structures, every level is accounted for. The generating rule explains WHY each element exists. No level is skipped. K(rule) = K(everything).

With continua + fundamental randomness (collapse QM), there's a gap:
- K(generating rule/distribution) is low
- K(specific random instantiation) is infinite (algorithmic randomness = incompressible)
- No intermediate level connects them
- The instruction "draw from distribution X" hides infinite K behind a short rule
- This is NOT like emergent randomness (leaves on a tree) where micro-determinism produces macro-randomness
- This is randomness AT CORE with no lower level to explain specific values

**Implication for the "saving continua" argument:**
The claim "randomness doesn't add to specification complexity" is only true if you measure K at the rule level and accept that the specific realization is unexplained. This is a missing level — the rule says what distribution, but not why THIS specific value. Under collapse interpretations, this gap is real.

**MWI dissolves the gap:**
Under Many-Worlds, no specific outcome is selected. All outcomes occur. The wavefunction (low K) determines everything. No branch needs specification. No infinite-K instantiation exists. The "missing level" only appears under collapse.

This STRENGTHENS the SDP argument for Many-Worlds: MWI is the only interpretation that avoids infinite-K at the instantiation level. Collapse introduces a level of specification (which outcome?) that has infinite K. MWI doesn't.

**Connection to discrete vs continuous:**
Discrete structures avoid the whole problem — every level accounted for, no missing explanatory step. This adds a NEW argument for discrete foundations beyond simplicity: discrete structures have no explanatory gaps. Continua + fundamental randomness have an explanatory gap that only MWI closes, and MWI is itself more probable under SDP (fewer postulates). The arguments reinforce each other.

**Status:** New insight. May warrant softening the "saving continua" fix or adding caveat about interpretation-dependence. For P6 and Book3, add: "This saving works cleanly under Many-Worlds where all outcomes occur and no specific value needs specification. Under collapse interpretations, the specific random outcome has infinite K, reintroducing the problem."

## Research Note: Constructive vs Axiomatic Existence

**Key distinction (from session):**
- Discrete structures: every element placed by rule. Rule determines AND enumerates. Self-contained. No background presupposed.
- Deterministic continua: rule determines what happens at every point but can't enumerate them. Presupposes the real line as background. The circle equation doesn't create the plane — it constrains a plane that must already exist from axioms (ZFC constructs the reals).
- Random continua: rule determines distribution but not specific values. Explanatory gap. Infinite K at instantiation under collapse; dissolved by MWI.

**The question:** is "exists by axiom" (continuum points exist because ZFC says all reals exist) ontologically weaker than "exists by construction" (discrete nodes exist because a rule placed them)?

Under matheism: if all consistent structures exist, then the axioms of ZFC are consistent, so the reals exist. But the reals exist as a structure, not as individually placed points. No rule said "put a point at 0.7341928." The axioms said "all reals exist" and that position is a consequence.

Is this a meaningful distinction? Constructive mathematicians (Brouwer, Bishop) say yes: only what can be constructed in finite steps truly exists. Classical mathematicians say no: consistency suffices for existence.

Matheism says all consistent structures exist — which seems to side with classical math. But SDP weights by K of generating rules. The reals have finite K (ZFC axioms are short). Individual real numbers that are algorithmically random have infinite K. The structure exists. Most of its individual elements can't be specified.

**Connection to points/shapes insight:** discrete nodes don't have the "what is it made of" problem as badly because each one is individually accounted for by the generating rule. Continuum points DO have it: uncountably many exist by axiomatic fiat, individually unspecifiable.

**Open question:** does this mean discrete structures are more "real" under SDP even when both have finite K at the rule level? The discrete rule has finite K AND finite enumeration. The continuous rule has finite K but infinite enumeration. Is that a meaningful measure difference?

**Status:** genuinely open. Do not overclaim in submission files. Current treatment (discrete likely wins on parsimony + explanatory completeness, continua not categorically excluded) is the right level of honesty.
